Mission Briefing · Before we write

You are about to build a paragraph out of someone else's ideas.

Not copy it. Build it — plan it, organize it, write it, then make it stronger. Real writers almost never start with sentence one. They start with a process. That process is what this mission teaches you, and you will use it on every text you read this year.

Foreword

What writing actually is: The Writing Rope

Writing looks like one skill. It isn't. Literacy researcher Joan Sedita describes skilled writing as a rope — five separate strands twisted together. When writing feels hard, it is almost never "I'm bad at writing." It is usually one strand that needs work. Tap each strand to see what it does.

Why this matters for today

Today's mission works mostly on Critical Thinking (what do I want to say?) and Text Structure (how do I arrange it?). Those are the two strands the POWeR Cycle is built to strengthen. We'll touch Syntax and Writing Craft in the mini-lessons, and Transcription when you edit.

The Plan

The POWeR Cycle — your repeatable process

Every text you read this year gets the same treatment. Once you know these five moves, you can write about anything: a science article, a novel, a primary source, a test prompt.

The 80 / 20 rule (from your teacher's playbook)

The first time through, the teacher does about 80% of the thinking out loud and you do 20%. By the end of the year that flips: you do 80% and the teacher does 20%. This game is the "I do / we do" version — it walks beside you. The paper version later is all you.

Day 1 · Prepare to write

Power up your self-talk 🔋

Writing is the hardest thing we ask your brain to do. It asks you to think of an idea, hold it in your memory, choose words for it, spell those words, and punctuate them — all at once. So of course it feels hard. Feeling stuck is not evidence that you are a bad writer. It's evidence that you're writing.

The move

Skilled writers don't avoid the stuck feeling — they talk themselves through it. That inner voice is a tool you can actually change. Below are things students really say when writing gets hard. Your job: swap each one for something that keeps you working.

Day 1 · Pre-read

Read it once. For real.

This first read has no job attached — no highlighting, no notes, no organizer. Just read it the way you'd read something interesting. Writers who skip this read end up writing about the one sentence they happened to notice instead of what the article is actually about.

P1 · Plan

Get the Gist 🧠

The gist is the whole article squeezed into one sentence, in your own words. It is the single most useful reading skill there is — and it is the thing most students skip.

Mini-lesson · Gist ≠ retell

A retell lists everything that happened. A gist names what the whole thing is about. If your gist only works for one paragraph of the article, it's too narrow. If it would work for any article ever written about science, it's too broad.

✗ Too narrow: “A monkey moved a robot arm in Japan.”
✗ Too broad: “This article is about technology.”
✓ Just right: names who/what, did what, when/where, and why/what happened.

P2 · Plan

Pull apart the prompt 🔍

More points are lost to not answering the question than to bad writing. Before you write one word, you break the prompt into DO (the action verbs) and WHAT (the job each verb points to).

Mini-lesson · Prompts usually have more than one job

Read a prompt like a checklist, not a paragraph. Every verb is a task you must actually perform. Miss one verb, and you can write beautifully and still only earn half credit. Also watch for the setup sentence — the sentence that gives you context but doesn't ask you to do anything.

P3 · Plan

Pick your ideas 🖍️

Now — and only now — you go back into the text with a highlighter. You are not hunting for "interesting." You are hunting for sentences that do a specific job: show a way bionic technology improves someone's life.

Mini-lesson · The evidence filter

Before you highlight a sentence, ask: “Does this prove my point, or is it just cool?” The article is full of fascinating sentences that would earn you zero points on this prompt, because they're about the debate over bionics, not the benefit of bionics. Highlight with your prompt in front of you.

And record the page number as you go. Hunting for it later costs you five minutes you don't have.

O · Organize

Build your TIDE-L 🌊

This is the skeleton of your paragraph. You are not writing sentences yet — you are placing the bones in order. Teachers call these notes “jot talk”: short, messy, fast. If you write full polished sentences here you'll be too attached to change them later.

The shape you're building

T topic → I evidence → D explain → I → D → I → D → E ending, with a L language box on the side. Notice the rhythm: every piece of evidence gets an explanation. Never leave an I standing alone.

Why I → D, always

A quote does not speak for itself. The D is where you show up in the writing — it's the only part of the paragraph that is 100% your thinking. Graders look for it first.
